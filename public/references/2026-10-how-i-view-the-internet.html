Why it's here

A worldview essay from someone who has made more than a thousand videos. Its sharpest argument is "stop making content, start making formats", and it makes that case from the audience's side rather than as a productivity tip.

What it is

A long Substack essay published 24 Sep 2026. It marks the end of his Create.Repeat newsletter and the start of THE INTERNET IS PEOPLE. It is a series of short principles, each under its own heading. The PDF was uploaded on 2026-10-03 and is not kept in this repo (see README).

The argument, section by section

  • The internet is people. "The algorithm wants what people want." The ranking system just collects lots of small human choices, such as whether someone stopped, stayed or shared. Strategy is art (making it), science (watching what happened) and psychology (asking why). The one question that matters: why would a person care?
  • Attention is currency. Distribution used to be scarce. Now attention is. "Effort does not equal attention", so the audience owes you nothing. The useful question is not "why didn't they watch mine?" but "why did millions choose that?"
  • The idea matters more than almost anything. Ask what is the premise? Good premises compress into one sentence, sometimes five words. His examples are Hot Ones, SubwayTakes and trivia in a taxi. "If I need five minutes to explain why the idea is good… I don't have the idea yet."
  • Clarity beats cleverness. The scroller decides what is this, and why should I care? almost instantly. Titles, thumbnails, hooks and first frames are part of the story, not added afterwards: "packaging is storytelling."
  • Curiosity gets the click. Satisfaction earns the audience. He describes a chain: promise, then curiosity, then delivery, then satisfaction, then trust. After enough satisfaction people stop judging each piece on its own ("I know what I'm getting here").
  • Stop making content. Start making formats. Thinking post by post means starting from zero every morning. The better question is what can we make that people will want again? A viral video is an event, while a repeatable format is an asset. "People love shows."
  • Familiarity and novelty. "A new surprise inside the same container." His test: could we make 1,000 episodes of this?
  • Consistency is really consistency of promise. It is less about how often you post and more about knowing what am I signing up for? Audiences subscribe to promises, not accounts.
  • The internet organises around interests. You don't need everyone, only the people who care a lot.
  • Creators are becoming networks. The shift is from "people who post" to "people who program", with producers, editors, development and IP around them.
  • Short form is discovery, long form is relationship. His funnel: short form brings discovery, long form builds a relationship, repeatable formats build a habit, community builds loyalty, and products or IP turn that into a business. "Your viral TikTok… might just be the front door."
  • Stop trying to hack the algorithm. Hacks are tactics. What lasts is "immediate feedback at enormous scale". Listen to it without blindly obeying it, and always ask what are we going to do differently next time?
  • The best internet teams behave like scientists. Hypothesis, make, observe, learn: "Create. Learn. Repeat." Doing this many times builds intuition, which he calls "a way of seeing".
  • Close. "The internet isn't random. It is a giant, constantly evolving experiment in human attention." Platforms change, but people change slowly.

Notes

  • His three questions run through the whole piece: why would a person care? what is the premise? could we make 1,000 episodes of this?
  • It is written for anyone whose job is putting something in front of an audience, not only creators. That includes editors, producers and brands, which fits how the club is made up.

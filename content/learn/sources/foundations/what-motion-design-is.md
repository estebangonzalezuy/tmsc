Most people arrive at motion design through a piece of software. They see
something move, they find out it was made in After Effects, and the first
question becomes "how do I learn After Effects".

I think that is the wrong first question, and it costs people about a year.

## The job is timing, not tooling

Motion design is deciding what a viewer notices, in what order, and how long
they get with each thing. That is the whole job. The software is where you go
to write the decision down.

You can tell because the decision survives the tool. A logo that reveals well
in After Effects reveals well in Rive, in Blender, in a CSS transition. The
timing is the design. Everything else is typing.

:::note
This is also why watching a tutorial feels productive and changes nothing. You
are watching someone type. The decisions were made before the recording
started.
:::

## Three things that carry across everything

- **Order.** Something moves first. Something waits. If everything arrives at
  once, nothing was emphasised.
- **Speed.** How long a thing takes says what it weighs. Slow reads heavy.
  Fast reads cheap, or urgent, depending on what came before it.
- **Rest.** The frames where nothing moves are doing work. Beginners fill
  them, and the piece turns into noise.

Learn those three and you can pick up any tool in a fortnight. Learn a tool
first and you will make the same piece over and over in it.

## So what do you do today

Pick something you already designed. A poster, a logo, a screen. Do not open
any software yet.

:::do minutes=20
Write three words for how it should feel. Then write, in plain sentences, what
moves first, what waits, and where it rests. Three sentences is enough.
:::

That paragraph is your brief. Every day after this one is about getting better
at writing it, and then at hitting it.

> Fundamentals are permanent. Tools are exhausting.

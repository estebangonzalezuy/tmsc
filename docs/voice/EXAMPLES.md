### POST 1 — The reference folder

📁 One habit that changed how I work: saving references.

Not Pinterest boards with 500 pins you never look at again. A folder on your desktop with 10-15 pieces of motion you actually like.

Every time I start a new project, I open that folder first. Not to copy. To remember what good looks like before I start making decisions.

It takes 2 minutes. And it's the difference between starting from a feeling and starting from a blank screen.

I suggest trying exactly that. Start small: save 5 things this week that made you stop scrolling.

### POST 2 — Easing is everything

⏱ If I could only teach one thing about motion, it would be easing.

The difference between amateur and professional motion is almost always in the curves. Linear motion (constant speed from A to B) feels robotic. Nobody moves like that. Nothing in nature moves like that.

Good easing makes things feel alive. A logo that settles into place with a slight overshoot. A transition that accelerates and then slows down before landing.

Spend a full day just playing with easing curves on a simple shape. Move a circle from left to right with 20 different curves. Watch how each one feels completely different.

That's the exercise. Simple and boring. But if you get this right, everything else gets better.

### POST 3 — We're designers

🔧 Something I think about a lot: motion designers are designers, not artists.

I know that's uncomfortable for some people. But I think the distinction matters.

An artist expresses something personal. A designer solves a problem for someone else. Both are valid. But when you're doing client work, you're designing. There's a brief, a context, a purpose.

The artistic part shows up in how you solve it. Your taste, your choices, your eye. But the starting point is always the problem, not the expression.

I find that freeing, actually. It takes the pressure off "being creative" and puts it on "being useful."

### POST 6 — The scroll test

👁 Here's something I do when I finish a piece:

I put it on my phone. I scroll past it like I would on Instagram or LinkedIn. Fast.

Did it catch my eye? Did I understand what it was about in 2 seconds? Did the movement feel right at that speed?

If yes, it probably works.

If I had to slow down and pay attention to get it, it probably needs another pass.

Most motion work lives on a feed now. Not on a big screen in a presentation room. Designing for the scroll is just designing for reality.

### POST 7 — The "fun" question

🤔 I see a lot of motion designers posting "Working on this was so much fun!"

I don't think fun is the word I'd use for work.

Sometimes work is interesting. Sometimes it's engaging. Sometimes you get focused because you're solving something you care about. But fun? I find fun playing beach volleyball. Not sitting in front of After Effects at midnight fixing an easing curve.

I think calling work "fun" hides the real thing: it was hard, you were good at it, and the result came out well. That's satisfying. Satisfying and fun are different.

Maybe this is just me. But I'd rather be honest about what the work actually feels like.

### POST 8 — The project that teaches you

🔑 The project that teaches you the most is rarely the one with the biggest budget.

It's usually the one where the brief was tight, the timeline was short, and you had to make fast decisions with limited options.

Those constraints force you to rely on fundamentals. You can't spend 3 days exploring effects. You animate the thing, you make it work, you ship it.

I've learned more from 3-day projects than from 3-month projects. The short ones show you what you actually know vs. what you need the internet for.

### POST 9 — Watching motion

👀 An exercise I do sometimes that I think is useful for anyone learning motion:

Watch a piece of motion you like. Watch it 3 times.

First time: just feel it. Do you like it? What catches your attention?

Second time: try to count the number of distinct movements. How many things are moving? In what order?

Third time: focus on timing. Where is the motion fast? Where is it slow? Where does it pause?

This trains your eye more than any tutorial. Because you start seeing choices instead of just effects.

You can do this with any piece. Ads, title sequences, app transitions, even loading animations.

### POST 10 — Barcelona morning

☀️ I usually get my best work done before 9 AM.

The house is quiet, my head is clear, and I haven't seen anyone else's work yet so I'm not comparing.

Then the day starts. Emails, messages, life things. And the focus slowly fades.

I used to fight that. I tried to be productive all day. Now I just protect the morning and accept that the afternoon is for everything else.

If you're working on something creative on the side (learning motion, building a portfolio, starting something), maybe the trick is not finding "more time" but finding your best 2 hours and guarding them.

For me it's early morning. For you it might be late at night or a Sunday block.

The important part is knowing when your brain is sharpest and giving that time to the work that matters most.

Then you open a blank project and you're back to zero.

This is something I see a lot with designers learning motion. The tutorial teaches you the steps, but not the thinking that connects one decision to the next.

That gap between "I can follow along" and "I can make decisions on my own" is where the real learning happens. And it rarely comes from another tutorial.

It comes from practice with intention. Doing a brief, making choices, looking at the result, understanding what worked.

### POST 12 — What studios actually look for

👀 I've worked with studios across Europe for a few years now.

One thing I noticed: they almost never ask what software you use.

They look at your work and they either feel it or they don't. The timing, the rhythm, the choices. Whether the motion feels intentional or random.

That's the thing that's hard to teach yourself from YouTube. Because the tutorials focus on the how (this effect, this plugin, this expression) and skip the why (why does this move this way, why this timing, why this composition).

I don't think there's a shortcut for developing that eye. But there's a faster path than figuring it out alone for 3 years.

Practice, references, and someone who can point at your work and say "this part works, this part doesn't, here's why."

### POST 13 — The five areas

🧩 If I had to break down commercial motion design into categories, I'd say there are five main areas where most of the work lives:

1. Fundamentals (rhythm, timing, composition, easing)
2. Branding in motion (making an identity move)
3. UI and product (micro-interactions, transitions)
4. Digital ads (short, high-impact narrative)
5. Showcasing (how you present your work)

Most self-taught designers go deep on 1-2 of these and skip the rest. Which makes sense, you learn what you need for the project in front of you.

But having at least a basic understanding of all five makes you way more flexible. And more hireable.

I'm curious: which of these five do you feel strongest in? And which one feels like a gap?

### POST 14 — Fundamentals are boring (and permanent)

🪨 Nobody gets excited about rhythm and timing.

Easing curves, spacing, composition. These are the boring parts.

But every piece of motion work I've done that actually felt good came back to these things. And every piece that felt off, I could trace the problem back to one of them.

Tools change every year. Fundamentals have been the same since Disney's animators wrote them down.

I think there's something calming about that. You don't need to chase the latest thing. You need to get good at the thing that doesn't change.

### POST 15 — The graphic designer question

✏️ A graphic designer asked me last week: "I want to learn motion but I don't know where to start. Should I learn After Effects first?"

My answer: not yet.

Before opening any software, I think it helps to spend a few days just watching motion. Watch title sequences, watch brand animations, watch UI transitions. But don't just watch. Try to describe what you see.

Why does this feel smooth? Why does that feel rushed? Where does your eye go?

This is what I call developing your "ojo clínico" (a trained eye, basically). It's the thing that makes the difference between someone who can animate and someone who can make good motion.

Software comes after. And it comes faster when you already know what you're trying to do with it.

### POST 16 — Practice vs. learning

🏐 I play beach volleyball. Not well, but I play.

Watching tutorials = watching volleyball videos.
Doing briefs and getting feedback = actually playing.

Both are useful. But only one of them actually changes your work.

### POST 17 — What I wish I knew earlier

🌱 Things I wish someone told me when I was starting in motion design:

You don't need to learn every tool. Pick one and go deep.

Your first 20 projects will not be great. That's fine. Do them anyway.

Fundamentals feel basic but they're what separate you from 80% of people applying for the same jobs.

### POST 18 — Branding in motion

🎨 A brand identity that doesn't move is incomplete in 2026.

But "adding motion to a brand" is not just making the logo spin. Or bounce. Or fade in.

The movement has to feel like the brand. A luxury brand moves differently than a tech startup. A children's brand moves differently than a financial institution.

This is one of the areas where I see designers struggle the most. Because it requires understanding both the brand (strategy, tone, personality) and the motion (timing, rhythm, energy).

It's a small skill. But studios pay well for it because most people can't do it.

### POST 19 — The portfolio problem

📂 I've helped a few designers clean up their portfolios. The same pattern always shows up:

Too many pieces. Not enough explanation. No sense of process.

A strong portfolio is not "here's everything I've ever made." It's "here are 5-8 pieces that show how I think."

Each piece should answer: What was the brief? What decisions did I make? Why?

Studios don't just want to see that you can animate. They want to see that you can think through a problem and arrive at something intentional.

Less work. More context. That's usually the fix.
